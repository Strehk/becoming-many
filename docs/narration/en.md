# Narration Script — English

## Prologue

There you are.

Look around, if you like. There's nothing to find.

This isn't an empty room. In front of you, air is moving. Particles drifting. This space is full
with things happening — we simply don't perceive most of them.

Think, for a moment, about what you call the world. Colour. Sound. The weight of things in your
hand. It feels like reality itself — complete, obvious, simply as it is.

But none of it reaches you directly. You encounter the world through your senses. What you
experience is the world as your body is able to perceive it.

## Chemical Sense — Scent

We treat smells as if they belong to things. The rose smells, the coffee smells. But once
released, scent becomes part of the air itself.

It's a cloud that moves, breaks apart, reassembles, gets dragged across the room by the wind.

To an animal that navigates by smell, air is not emptiness. It's a landscape of plumes and
currents — a map redrawn every second.

## Bat — Depth

Hey, did you catch the bat? Let's give this world some bat-like context.

Near and far. Distance, shape, space.

A world where the first question isn't what is that — but how far is that?

## Insect — Motion

Did you see the insects? Now follow your own attention. It jumps toward anything that stirs in
the sky. You can hardly help it.

For many animals, what holds still is nearly invisible. What twitches is everything. A frog, for
example, can starve beside a pile of dead flies, yet snap at anything that moves.

For the frog, the motionless isn't an object at all. Only the living, the flickering, is real.

## Infrared

Do you sense this?

Some snakes have organs just below the eyes — small pits, lined with membrane so thin it
registers heat.

The signal runs into the same part of the brain that handles vision. There, it merges.

You can now also see the prey.

Because being alive means giving off heat, and heat cannot keep a secret.

## Migratory Bird — The Magnetic Field

Some ways to see the world are almost impossible to imagine.

Migratory birds fly thousands of kilometres south to overwinter. No map. No one to show them the
way. Yet they find it anyway — in part because they can sense the magnetic field of the Earth.

Some researchers think that certain birds can almost see it. As a kind of shimmer, a tint laid
over everything, showing them where north is.

## Finale

Look at what happens when it's all here at once. Depth. Motion. The magnetic field. The scent
moving through all of it.

And suddenly, you begin to see connections that were invisible before. One sense gives context
to another. What seemed separate at first, becomes part of the same world.

For a moment — you become many.

## Overload

But slowly — do you feel it?

Is it becoming too much for you? Too much world at once?

You may just have wondered why no animal lives like this. With all senses. It sounds like an
advantage.

Perhaps it would be. Or perhaps there is a reason evolution never took that path. Every sense
demands something of a being — nerves, energy, brain power. And above all: attention.

But where is the limit? Would perceiving more bring us closer to reality? Or would it simply
leave us with too much of it?

## Return

The space looks empty again. But you know better now.

None of what you saw has gone anywhere.

We tend to mistake what we perceive for the whole world. But what you call your reality isn't
all there is.

It's a beautiful, vital glimpse. One among countless others.

Look around one last time, before you go back.

Only air. Only wind. Only white.

And everything else — just underneath.
